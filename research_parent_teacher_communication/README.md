# The Importance of Parent-Teacher Communication in Early Childhood: A Research Review

## Executive Summary: Synthesis of Key Themes

Extensive academic research confirms that effective parent-teacher communication is not merely an administrative task but a critical pedagogical intervention. The literature consistently demonstrates that robust communication loops—specifically those that are **individualized, bi-directional, and frequent**—directly drive improved learning outcomes for children and significantly enhance parental satisfaction with the school.

### 1. Communication Drives Learning Outcomes via "Home Engagement"
Research indicates that the primary mechanism by which schools improve early childhood outcomes is by activating the home environment.
*   **The Mediation Model:** A 2020 study by **Barnett et al.** found that Early Childhood Education (ECE) engagement practices (specifically sending information home) effectively increase "home learning activities" (e.g., reading, stimulating play). It is this increase in home activity that directly correlates with higher **kindergarten academic readiness**.
*   **Reducing Academic Failure:** In a landmark field experiment, **Kraft & Rogers (2015)** demonstrated that sending weekly, one-sentence individualized messages to parents reduced course failure rates by **41%**. This intervention worked by empowering parents to have more specific, improvement-oriented conversations with their children.
*   **Social-Behavioral Competence:** A systematic review by **See et al. (2020)** found that **bi-directional** communication (two-way dialogue) had a significant positive impact on students' social-behavioral competence, whereas one-way communication (broadcasts) did not.

### 2. Digital Tools Enable "Proactive Involvement"
Modern research highlights the specific utility of digital platforms in bridging the gap between school and home.
*   **Visual Proof of Learning:** **Chen & Rivera-Vernazza (2022)** found that sharing photos and videos via apps serves as a "springboard" for parent-child conversations. Parents reported that seeing visual evidence of classroom activities allowed them to engage their children in meaningful recall and reinforcement of what was learned, transforming a passive "how was your day?" into an active educational dialogue.
*   **Overcoming Barriers:** Digital communication allows for "proactive involvement" (home-based support) even when parents cannot physically volunteer at the school due to work schedules, effectively democratizing parental engagement (Chen, 2022).

### 3. Communication is the Key Driver of Parent Satisfaction
For marketing purposes, the research supports the claim that communication is a primary determinant of how parents perceive school quality.
*   **Predicting Satisfaction:** Research by **Friedman et al. (2007)** and **Fantuzzo et al. (2006)** identified "receiving information from the school" and "teacher contact" as top independent predictors of overall parental satisfaction. Parents perceive schools as "higher quality" when they receive consistent, personalized updates.
*   **Trust and Partnership:** **Galindo & Sheldon (2012)** established that school-home connections foster a sense of partnership that mediates achievement gains. Parents who feel connected are more likely to trust the school's pedagogical approach and remain enrolled.

## Detailed Summaries of Key Papers

### 1. The Underutilized Potential of Teacher-to-Parent Communication: Evidence from a Field Experiment
**Authors:** Matthew A. Kraft & Todd Rogers (Harvard/Brown University)
**Publication:** *Economics of Education Review* (2015)

**The Study:** This randomized field experiment tested the impact of a low-cost communication intervention: sending weekly, one-sentence individualized messages from teachers to parents of high school students in a credit recovery program.
**Key Findings:**
*   **41% Reduction in Failure:** The intervention reduced the percentage of students failing to earn course credit from 15.8% to 9.3%.
*   **Mechanism of Action:** The messages shaped parent-child conversations. Parents receiving messages were more likely to discuss specific areas for improvement rather than general praise, which motivated student effort.
*   **Cost-Effectiveness:** The cost per additional credit earned was less than one-tenth of the typical district cost for credit recovery interventions.
**Relevance:** Demonstrates the massive ROI of frequent, short, digital "nudges" to parents.

### 2. Influences of Parent Engagement in Early Childhood Education Centers and the Home on Kindergarten School Readiness
**Authors:** Melissa A. Barnett et al. (University of Arizona)
**Publication:** *Early Childhood Research Quarterly* (2020)

**The Study:** Utilizing data from the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study (Birth Cohort), this study examined how ECE provider practices linked to kindergarten readiness for 4-year-olds.
**Key Findings:**
*   **Indirect Pathway:** The study confirmed a "mediation model." ECE communication practices (sending info home) did not just help the parent feel good; they caused the parent to engage in more **home learning activities**.
*   **Outcome:** These increased home learning activities were the direct cause of improved kindergarten academic readiness.
*   **Equity:** The positive link between school communication and home engagement was strongest for low-income families, suggesting communication apps are a powerful equity tool.
**Relevance:** Scientifically proves that "sending info home" is a pedagogical strategy that boosts school readiness.

### 3. Communicating Digitally: Building Preschool Teacher-Parent Partnerships Via Digital Technologies During COVID-19
**Authors:** Jennifer J. Chen & Dahana E. Rivera-Vernazza (Kean University)
**Publication:** *Early Childhood Education Journal* (2022)

**The Study:** A qualitative investigation into how digital tools (apps, text, email) were used to maintain partnerships when physical access to schools was restricted.
**Key Findings:**
*   **The "Springboard" Effect:** Parents reported that photos and videos sent via apps provided a specific context ("I saw you painting a blue boat today") that allowed them to have rich conversations with their non-communicative toddlers/preschoolers.
*   **Proactive Involvement:** The study defined a shift from "reactive involvement" (attending meetings) to "proactive involvement" (supporting learning at home), which is facilitated primarily by digital communication.
**Relevance:** Validates the specific features of communication apps (media sharing) as educational tools that extend the classroom into the home.

### 4. A Systematic Review of the Impact of Technology-Mediated Parental Engagement on Student Outcomes
**Authors:** See et al. (Durham University)
**Publication:** *Educational Research and Evaluation* (2020)

**The Study:** A rigorous review of 29 studies to determine which types of digital parental engagement actually improve student outcomes.
**Key Findings:**
*   **Bi-directional vs. One-way:** The review found that simple "one-way" communication (newsletters, broadcasts) had little measurable impact on outcomes. However, **bi-directional** communication (where parents can reply or dialogue) had a significant positive impact on students' **social-behavioral competence**.
**Relevance:** Supports the argument for two-way messaging apps over static websites or email blasts.

## Annotated Bibliography

**Barnett, M. A., Paschall, K. W., Mastergeorge, A. M., Cutshaw, C. A., & Warren, S. M. (2020).** *Influences of parent engagement in early childhood education centers and the home on kindergarten school readiness.* **Early Childhood Research Quarterly, 53, 260–273.**
> A quantitative study analyzing national longitudinal data. It establishes a causal chain: School Communication → Increased Home Engagement → Improved School Readiness. It is particularly notable for finding that this effect is strongest in lower-income households.

**Chen, J. J., & Rivera-Vernazza, D. E. (2022).** *Communicating digitally: Building preschool teacher-parent partnerships via digital technologies during COVID-19.* **Early Childhood Education Journal, 50, 1–15.**
> A qualitative study focusing on the user experience of digital communication tools in preschools. It highlights the unique value of photo/video sharing in sparking parent-child dialogue and maintaining "partnership" when face-to-face interaction is limited.

**Fantuzzo, J., Perry, M. A., & Childs, S. (2006).** *Parent Satisfaction with Educational Experiences Scale.* **School Psychology Review, 35(3), 371-397.**
> Validates the dimensions of parent satisfaction, identifying "Teacher Contact" as a core dimension. This supports the marketing claim that better communication leads to higher perceived school quality.

**Friedman, B. A., Bobrowski, P. E., & Markow, D. (2007).** *Predictors of parents' satisfaction with their children's school.* **Journal of Educational Administration, 45(3), 278-288.**
> Identifies "receiving information from the school" as a primary driver of parental satisfaction, linking administrative communication practices directly to the "customer satisfaction" aspect of school management.

**Galindo, C., & Sheldon, S. B. (2012).** *School and home connections and children’s kindergarten achievement gains: The mediating role of family involvement.* **Early Childhood Research Quarterly, 27(1), 90–103.**
> Demonstrates that school efforts to connect with families result in higher student achievement in reading and math, mediated by the family's increased involvement in the child's education.

**Kraft, M. A., & Rogers, T. (2015).** *The underutilized potential of teacher-to-parent communication: Evidence from a field experiment.* **Economics of Education Review, 47, 49–63.**
> A field experiment proving that frequent, short, individualized messages from teachers to parents can reduce student course failure rates by 41%. It provides hard ROI data for communication interventions.

**See, B. H., Gorard, S., El-Soufi, N., Lu, B., Siddiqui, N., & Dong, L. (2020).** *A systematic review of the impact of technology-mediated parental engagement on student outcomes.* **Educational Research and Evaluation, 26(3–4), 150–181.**
> A systematic review distinguishing between the effectiveness of different communication types. It strongly supports **bi-directional** communication over one-way information dissemination for improving social-behavioral outcomes.
